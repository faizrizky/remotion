## COLD OPEN (0:00 - 0:30) - file: `v3_00_cold.wav`
Same box. Same price. Same spot on the shelf.
But open it up... and there's less inside.

Nobody raised the price. Nobody sent you a memo.
The bag just... got lighter.

It's called shrinkflation. And it works because of a tiny bug in how your brain shops.
By the end of this video, you'll know where it hides, why it works so well...
and the one number on the shelf tag that catches it every single time.

*(brand sting: Penny Logics, ~4.75 s, opening music, no voice)*

## CHAPTER 1 - "The Invisible Price Hike" (0:30 - 1:10) - file: `v3_01_hike.wav`
When a company's costs go up, it has two options.
Option one: raise the price. Your four-dollar box becomes four-fifty... and you notice.
Option two: keep it at four dollars... and put less inside.

Same price. Less product. That's still a price increase. You just can't see it.
Economists call it shrinkflation. Shrink, plus inflation.

And it's not new.
In 2016, Toblerone in the UK widened the gaps between its famous triangles. The big bar dropped from four hundred grams to three hundred sixty. [S1]
The shape stayed iconic. The chocolate didn't.

## CHAPTER 2 - "Why Your Brain Misses It" (1:10 - 1:55) - file: `v3_02_brain.wav`
So why does option two work so well?
Because you check prices. You don't check weights.

Researchers studied years of ice cream sales in Chicago. They found shoppers react about four times more to a change in price than to the same change in package size. [S2]
Four times.

For a company, that makes the choice easy.
Raise the price, and some shoppers walk away.
Shrink the package, and most of them never notice.

And the package is built to help. Same colors. Same logo. Same size on the shelf.
Often the only thing that changes is one small number on the front: the net weight.

## CHAPTER 3 - "Where It Hides" (1:55 - 2:50) - file: `v3_03_hides.wav`
Once you start looking, you see it everywhere.
In 2022, NPR documented a whole wave of it in the US. [S3]

Cottonelle toilet paper: three hundred forty sheets per roll became three hundred twelve.
Kleenex: a box of sixty-five tissues became sixty.
Gatorade started swapping its thirty-two-ounce bottles for twenty-eight.
Folgers coffee went from fifty-one ounces to forty-three and a half... and the can still said it makes up to four hundred cups.
Domino's cut its wing order from ten pieces to eight, for the same seven ninety-nine.

And shrinkflation has a quieter cousin: skimpflation.
Same size, same price... but cheaper ingredients, or less service. [S4]
That one doesn't even show up on the label.

## CHAPTER 4 - "Does It Really Matter?" (2:50 - 3:45) - file: `v3_04_matter.wav`
Now, here's the honest part.
For the economy as a whole, shrinkflation is tiny.

In 2025, the US Government Accountability Office looked at prices from 2019 to 2024. Overall, prices rose about thirty-four and a half percent. Shrinking packages added less than a tenth of one percentage point of that. [S5]
So no, it's not why everything feels expensive.

But zoom in on your cart... and it's a different story.
For household paper products, like paper towels, shrinking packages added three full percentage points of inflation. For cereal, one point six. [S5]
And in cereal, only about one percent of products shrank... but those products made up almost nine percent of sales. [S5]

In other words, it doesn't hit random items.
It tends to hit the things people buy the most.

## CHAPTER 5 - "Some Countries Fight Back" (3:45 - 4:25) - file: `v3_05_fight.wav`
Some governments decided shoppers shouldn't have to play detective.

Since July 2024, large supermarkets in France must put a notice on products that shrank while the price per unit went up. Companies that break the rule can be fined up to fifteen thousand euros. [S6]
South Korea now requires companies to disclose it too: on the package, in the store, or online. [S7]

And in the US?
There's no national rule like that. Bills have been proposed, but none has passed. [S8]
So you're on your own.
Well... almost.

## CHAPTER 6 - "The Number That Catches It" (4:25 - 5:05) - file: `v3_06_number.wav`
Remember the one number on the shelf tag that catches it every time?
It's the unit price. The small print that tells you what you pay per ounce, per sheet, or per hundred grams.

The big price can stay frozen for years. The unit price can't hide.
When the package shrinks, the unit price jumps.

Only nine states, plus Washington, D.C., require stores to show it. In most states, it's not required at all. [S9]
So if it's there, read it. If it isn't, do the math on your phone.

Three habits:
One: compare unit prices, not shelf prices.
Two: when a package gets a new design, check the net weight.
Three: count what you actually use: sheets, scoops, pieces. Not boxes.

## OUTRO (5:05 - 5:25) - file: `v3_07_outro.wav`
Shrinkflation works because you're watching the wrong number.
The price on the front is the one they want you to see. The unit price is the one that tells the truth.

Here's your challenge: next time you shop, check one thing you buy every week. Then tell me in the comments... did it shrink?
