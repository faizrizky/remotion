## CHAPTER 1 - "The Gap" (0:25 - 1:25) - file: `v4_01_gap.wav`
It has a name: negative equity. [PILL: NEGATIVE EQUITY] Some people say "upside down" or "underwater."

Here's how it happens. A new car starts losing value the moment you drive it off the lot. It loses it fastest in the first few years.
Your loan balance goes down too. But slowly, especially early on, because most of each payment goes to interest.

So there's a stretch of time where the loan is bigger than the car.
Two lines on a chart: the car's value dropping fast, the loan balance dropping slow. Where the loan line sits above the car line, that gap is negative equity.

It's not a problem if you keep the car and keep paying. The gap matters at one moment: when you need to sell, trade in, or when the car is totaled or taken back.

And how many people end up in that gap?
Edmunds says it's been rising four years in a row. [S1]

## CHAPTER 2 - "The Long Loan" (1:25 - 2:30) - file: `v4_02_long.wav`
[QCARD] Now the part that makes the gap bigger: how long people borrow for.

When a car gets more expensive, there's an easy way to keep the monthly payment from going up. Stretch the loan.

Take a forty-thousand-dollar loan at eight point one percent, which is the average rate in that Edmunds data. [S1]
Over sixty months, the payment is about eight hundred thirteen dollars.
Over eighty-four months, it's about six hundred twenty-five.

One hundred eighty-eight dollars less every month. That feels like a win. [PILL: -$188 a month]
But total interest goes from about eighty-seven hundred dollars to about twelve and a half thousand. That's nearly four thousand dollars more, for the same car. [S2] [PILL: +$3,759 in interest]

A longer loan lowers the payment and slows down how fast you build equity.
And it's common. Among buyers who rolled old debt into a new loan, more than ninety-one percent chose terms longer than six years. [S1]

There's another catch. A seven-year loan can outlast the warranty. Ford's basic warranty, for example, covers three years or thirty-six thousand miles. Hyundai's runs five years. Some brands cover the engine and transmission for up to ten years, but not every part. [S6]
So you can still be making payments after the repair coverage is gone.

## CHAPTER 3 - "Rolled Over" (2:30 - 3:45) - file: `v4_03_roll.wav`
[QCARD] So back to the gap. Where does it go?

For many people, it goes into the next loan.
You trade in the car you owe seven thousand on. The dealer pays off the old loan... and adds the leftover to the new one.

That's called rolling over negative equity. [PILL: ROLLED OVER] And look at what it does to the next loan. [S1]
The average rolled-over loan in the third quarter: fifty-seven thousand two hundred thirty-five dollars.
The monthly payment: nine hundred fifty-two dollars. At eight point one percent interest.
Over the life of that loan, interest alone: sixteen thousand six hundred ten dollars.

Compared to the average new-car buyer, those drivers borrowed about twelve thousand six hundred dollars more, pay about one hundred sixty-five dollars more a month, and are on track to pay about six thousand seven hundred more in interest. [S1]

And here's the twist. The new loan starts upside down from day one. So the gap doesn't go away. It travels to the next car.

## CHAPTER 4 - "When Payments Stop" (3:45 - 4:45) - file: `v4_04_stop.wav`
[QCARD] What happens when someone can't keep up?
[CHIP: DATE Q2 2026]

Start with the big picture. Americans owe about one point seven trillion dollars on auto loans, according to the New York Fed's report for the second quarter. [S3]
About three percent of that balance moved into serious delinquency, meaning ninety days or more behind, over the past year. [S3]
The New York Fed's own words: new delinquencies for auto loans "remain at elevated levels." [S3]

At the riskier end, the pattern looks worse. One data firm that tracks pools of subprime car loans reported that in September, about seven percent were sixty or more days behind. That's roughly double the level in September 2019. [S4]

And if a car is taken back, the story may not end there. If the lender sells it for less than you owe, in many states you can still be asked to pay the difference. [S5]
A repossession can also stay on your credit report for seven years, counted from the first missed payment. [S7]

## CHAPTER 5 - "The Honest Part" (4:45 - 5:30) - file: `v4_05_honest.wav`
[QCARD] So is this a crisis? Let's keep it in proportion.

Most car owners keep paying on time. Most trade-ins are not underwater. Seventy-one percent are not. [S1]
Delinquency is elevated. The data doesn't say a collapse is coming, and no source in this video says that. Anyone who tells you exactly what happens next is guessing.

What the numbers do show is simpler.
Cars cost more. Loans are longer. And more people are carrying yesterday's car debt into tomorrow's loan.

That's a decision you can see coming. And that means you can avoid it.

## CHAPTER 6 - "What You Can Do" (5:30 - 6:15) - file: `v4_06_do.wav`
[QCARD] Remember the one number to check today?
It's this: what you owe, minus what the car is worth.

Get your payoff amount from your lender. Get an estimated value from a valuation tool. Subtract.
If it's positive, you have equity. If it's negative, that's your gap.

Then three habits:
One: before you shop, shorten the loan if you can afford the payment. Six years or less is a good ceiling to consider.
Two: don't roll the gap into a new loan if there's any other option. Even a small extra payment toward the old loan shrinks it.
Three: if you're stuck, keeping the car and paying it down can beat trading it in at a loss.

This is education, not a recommendation for your situation. Your numbers are different from the average.
