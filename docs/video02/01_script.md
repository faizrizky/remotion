# Video 02 - Script
**Working title:** Pay in 4 Is "Free." So Why Do Stores Pay for It?
**Language:** English (international audience) - **Target length:** ~5:45 at ~150 wpm (~880 words)
**Voice:** same narrator voice and settings as Video 01. Energetic but calm, small pause (0.3 s) before every number.

Retention plan (why people stay):
- 0:00 open loop #1: "who is paying?" -> answered at 1:05-1:55.
- 0:20 open loop #2: "one number they hope you never add up" -> paid off only in the outro (keeps people to the end).
- 1:50 open loop #3: "the real trap isn't overspending" -> paid off in Chapter 4.

Four payments of thirty dollars. No interest. No fees.
You tap the button, the sneakers ship, and you've paid... thirty bucks.

Here's the weird part. The companies that give you this for free are worth billions.

So if you're not paying for it... who is?
And why do stores happily hand over a cut of every sale, just to show you that little "Pay in 4" button?

By the end of this video, you'll know exactly what they're buying.
Hint: it's you.
And there's one number they really hope you never add up.

## CHAPTER 1 - "The Free Lunch" (0:28 - 1:05) - file: `v2_01_free.wav`
Buy now, pay later works like this.
You split a purchase into four equal payments, usually two weeks apart. The first one is due today. On the classic Pay in 4 plan, there's no interest at all.

And it's everywhere.
In 2025, sixteen percent of American adults used it, according to the Federal Reserve. [S1]
In a March 2026 survey, forty-seven percent of Americans said they've tried it at least once. [S2]

So it's popular. It's free. It feels like the smart move.
Hold that thought.

## CHAPTER 2 - "Who Really Pays" (1:05 - 1:55) - file: `v2_02_who.wav`
Here's the part most people never see.
Every time you choose Pay in 4, the store pays the lender a fee. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says it's typically two to four percent of the purchase. [S3]

Why would a store give that up?
Because of what happens next.
Klarna tells stores that merchants who add it see, on average, twenty percent more sales completed... forty percent bigger orders... and forty-six percent more repeat purchases. [S4]
Those are Klarna's own numbers, and they say results vary.

But read that again. Forty percent bigger orders.
The store isn't paying for a payment button. It's paying for a bigger cart.
And the bigger cart... is yours.

## CHAPTER 3 - "The Thirty-Dollar Illusion" (1:55 - 2:45) - file: `v2_03_illusion.wav`
Why does it work so well?
Because your brain doesn't see a hundred and twenty dollars. It sees thirty.

Thirty feels like lunch. A hundred and twenty feels like a decision.
So the hoodie goes in the cart. Then the matching cap. Each one is just... another small payment.

This isn't just a feeling. Harvard researchers, in a paper published by the National Bureau of Economic Research, found that when people get access to buy now, pay later, their total spending goes up. It doesn't just move around. [S5]
And in a 2026 survey, sixty-eight percent of users agreed it makes them overspend. More than half have regretted a purchase. [S2]

But overspending once isn't the real trap.
The real trap is what happens when the thirty-dollar payments start stacking.

## CHAPTER 4 - "The Stack" (2:45 - 3:40) - file: `v2_04_stack.wav`
One Pay in 4 is easy to track.
Now open five.

The CFPB studied borrowers' records and found that sixty-three percent had more than one of these loans at the same time. About one in three had loans with more than one company. [S6]

Each app only sees its own loans. You're the only one who sees the whole pile... if you're keeping track at all.
That's starting to change: in 2025, FICO introduced credit scores that can include buy now, pay later data. [S7]

And it's not just sneakers anymore.
Twenty-nine percent of users say they've used it for groceries. Two years earlier, it was fourteen percent. [S2]
When you're splitting your food into four payments, that's not a shopping hack anymore.
That's a warning light.

## CHAPTER 5 - "When It Bites" (3:40 - 4:35) - file: `v2_05_bites.wav`
So what happens when a payment fails?
Most plans pull the money automatically from your card or bank account. If the money isn't there, you can get hit twice: a late fee from the lender... and an overdraft fee from your bank.

In the US, Klarna's late fee can be up to seven dollars. Afterpay's, up to eight, capped at a quarter of the order. [S8][S9]
Sounds small. On a thirty-dollar payment, it isn't.

And it happens a lot.
Forty-seven percent of users paid late at least once in the past year. [S2]
The Federal Reserve found twenty-one percent paid a late fee or got hit with an overdraft fee. Among people using it for groceries, forty-three percent. [S1]

One more thing. Not every plan is interest-free. The longer "pay monthly" plans can charge up to about thirty-six percent a year. [S10][S8]
Same app. Very different deal.

## CHAPTER 6 - "Use It Without Getting Used" (4:35 - 5:20) - file: `v2_06_rules.wav`
So is buy now, pay later evil? No.
Used on purpose, it's a free, short loan. The trick is making sure you're the one using it.

Rule one: only use it for something you could pay for in full today.
Rule two: one plan at a time. No stacking.
Rule three: never for groceries, bills, or subscriptions.
Rule four: put every due date in your calendar, and set aside the money for all four payments before you tap.

## OUTRO (5:20 - 5:45) - file: `v2_07_outro.wav`
Remember the one number they hope you never add up?
Here it is: every installment you owe over the next six weeks. All of them. Added together.
That's your real bill.
Go add it up right now. If it surprises you... the system is working. Just not for you.
